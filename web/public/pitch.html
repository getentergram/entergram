THE PROBLEM · WHAT IT COSTS

Your agent is brilliant and amnesiac.

Every session it re-reads the same files, re-derives the same architecture, and re-asks questions your team answered six months ago. It doesn't know why auth moved to JWT, why that module is shaped the way it is, or what broke last time someone touched the payment path.

  • Re-reads the same files, every session
  • Re-derives the same architecture, every session
  • Re-asks questions the team already answered
That re-derivation is the hidden tax on every prompt.
THE PROBLEM, CONCRETELY

One question, asked a thousand times: “why is it built this way?”

Every non-trivial change runs into it. Here is the same question, answered two ways.

Today — the agent reconstructs

It greps the codebase. Opens twelve files. Reads a migration. Infers intent from a variable name. Burns twenty minutes and a large slice of the context window — then produces a plausible answer.

Sometimes the plausible answer is wrong, and it re-introduces the exact design that failed last year. Nobody notices until production does.

With Entergram — it recalls
$ recall "why is auth stateless here?" → We moved auth from sessions to JWT in May. Sessions forced a Redis dependency and blocked horizontal scaling. Redis left the auth path; p99 login latency fell 40%. Cookie sessions still exist under /legacy. source: PR #812 · @alice · 2026-05-14
“We tried X. It failed because Y. That's why we do Z.” One query, not twenty minutes.

THE FRONTIER · WHAT NOBODY HAS SOLVED

Current systems model the organization's state. None model its reasoning.

This is the most useful finding in the whole landscape. Across every vendor, the same six things are missing — and they are precisely the things a senior engineer or an operator actually needs.

Modelled well today
  • Documents and content
  • People and org structure
  • Entities and their relationships
  • Permissions and access
  • Workflows and tasks
  • Current state of systems
Structurally absent
  • Intent — what were we actually trying to achieve?
  • Tradeoffs — what did we give up, knowingly?
  • Rejected alternatives — what did we try that failed?
  • Confidence — how sure were we, and were we right?
  • Evolving belief — what changed our mind, and when?
  • Counterfactuals — what if we'd chosen the other path?
A graph of entities tells you what exists. It cannot tell you what was decided, or why.

DOMAIN · HOW A HOSPITAL COMPOSES ITS BRAIN

A hospital already runs the loop — on paper, in committees, and in people's heads.

Clinical medicine is arguably the most decision-dense industry there is, and it already has the ritual: morbidity & mortality review, formulary committees, protocol revision, root-cause analysis. What it lacks is a queryable record of why the protocol reads the way it does today.

What the brain ingests
  • Protocol and order-set revisions (what changed, and on whose authority)
  • M&M and root-cause findings — the institution's hard-won negatives
  • Formulary and device decisions, with the alternatives that lost
  • Incident reports and near-misses
  • Staffing and escalation policy changes
What it can then answer
"why does our sepsis pathway differ from the national guideline?" → Revised after a 2024 review: two escalation delays traced to the standard trigger threshold in our ED's staffing pattern. Alternative considered: adopt guideline unchanged + add a nurse-initiated override. Rejected — override compliance measured under 40% here. Outcome: time-to-antibiotic improved; revisit at next audit. source: M&M 2024-11 · Protocol Cmte minutes · owner: Dr. R
→

Governance is the entry ticket, not a feature. PHI means permission inheritance, audit trail, and lineage are preconditions. Local-first helps — the record can live inside the hospital's own boundary — but enterprise RBAC/SSO is a genuine prerequisite we have not built. This is a design-partner conversation, not a sale we could serve today.

→

The decision record is deliberately not clinical advice. It returns what this institution decided and why, with provenance — an institutional-memory tool for clinicians and committees, not a diagnostic or treatment recommender. That distinction is what keeps it outside the regulated-device question.

DOMAIN · HOW A SMALL HARDWARE COMPANY COMPOSES ITS BRAIN

A 60-person connected-hardware company: fewer people, higher cost per forgotten decision.

Take the archetype of a smart-lock/IoT company — firmware, mobile apps, cloud backend, a physical BOM, suppliers, and units already in customers' doors. Small teams are usually assumed not to need organizational memory. The opposite is true: with one or two people per domain, a single departure removes an entire body of reasoning, and hardware decisions are the most expensive kind to re-learn.

Where its decisions actually live
  • Firmware repo + OTA release history
  • Component and supplier choices — the BOM's argument
  • Field failures, RMA patterns, warranty claims
  • Certification and regional compliance decisions
  • Support tickets where the same root cause recurs
  • The Slack thread where the tradeoff was actually settled
The question that pays for it
"why are we on this BLE module and not the cheaper one?" → Evaluated both in 2024. The cheaper module cut ~$1.40/unit. Rejected: intermittent pairing failures below 0°C in field testing — a warranty-return risk in cold-climate markets. Revisit condition: vendor's rev-C silicon, if it clears the same cold-soak test. source: hardware review thread + field test log · 2024-08
→

Why small companies are a better wedge than they look. No knowledge-management function, no Glean deployment, no ontology team — and the pain lands on the founder or lead engineer personally. Adoption is one person deciding, not a committee.

→

The counter-argument, stated honestly. A 60-person company may believe it doesn't need this — the reasoning still fits in a few people's heads. The trigger isn't headcount, it's the first time a decision has to be re-derived after the person who made it left. That's when the record is worth what it costs.

ENTERPRISE ONBOARDING · THE TWO PLANES

MCP is the read side. It does nothing for ingest.

This is the distinction most of the category blurs. “We support MCP” is not an answer to “how do you get our Jira data.” Ingest and consumption are separate planes, with separate integration strategies and separate failure modes.

ENTERPRISE ONBOARDING · CONNECTOR STRATEGY

We will not build 200 connectors — and we don't need to.

Search platforms ship 275+ connectors because any document might answer any query. Decision memory needs the surfaces where rationale is actually stated — a small, predictable set. That's a structural advantage, not a gap.

T1
Build natively (~6)
The decision-dense surfaces: code review, issue trackers, chat threads where tradeoffs settle, ADR/design docs, meeting transcripts, incident tooling. Event-native via webhooks where available, not polling.

T3

A documented webhook + JSON schema + SDK so the customer's platform team wires their bespoke internal systems. Every enterprise has a tool no vendor will ever connect to; the answer is a stable contract.

ENTERPRISE ONBOARDING · HOW THE FOUR BRAINS INTERACT

Read down. Promote up. Never copy.

Higher scopes are views over lower ones plus their own cells — not duplicated stores. Two rules make that tractable at enterprise scale.

Permissions are mirrored, never re-invented. Visibility derives from the source system's ACL — repo access, Slack channel membership, Confluence space permissions. Asking an enterprise to re-model its access control inside our product is both a security risk and a deployment blocker. Cross-scope effect is the product value: a gotcha promoted to team scope stops three colleagues repeating it; a product decision at org scope is what lets finance attribute cost.

Because the memory has to outlive the agent. A team on Claude Code today was on Copilot two years ago and may be on an internal agent next year. Every one of those migrations throws away vendor-held memory — which is precisely the asset that should have been compounding.

Agents will keep changing. The organisation's memory shouldn't have to.

GOVERNANCE · A FIRST-CLASS LAYER, NOT A FEATURE

Decision memory is the most sensitive data in the company.

A record of what an organization decided, what it rejected, and what it got wrong is more sensitive than the code it produced. Every serious platform in this space treats permissions, lineage and audit as architecture rather than configuration — mirroring source-system permissions instead of copying data into an unrestricted index.

→

Local-first by default. Cells are plain files in the customer's own repo. Nothing leaves the machine unless sync is explicitly configured — the strongest possible answer to “where does our decision history live?”

→

Provenance on every fact. Each cell carries its source — PR, commit, thread, or document — so a recalled claim is auditable rather than asserted.

→

Reviewable before trusted. Auto-extracted memory starts low-confidence and passes through human review; the store is plain Markdown, diffable in a normal PR.

→

Honest gap. Enterprise-grade RBAC/ABAC, SSO and permission inheritance are not built. That is a real requirement for large-org deployment and is on the roadmap, not in the product.

CELL B-0019 · A SELLING POINT, NOT AN AFTERTHOUGHT

Nothing leaves the machine by default.

→

Local-first is a sales feature, not just a policy — enterprises can't send proprietary code to mem0 or any cloud memory tool.

→

Extraction runs against a local model or the user's own API key; the cell store is plain local files.

→

entergram.toml declares what's in scope — never .env, secrets, or node_modules.

→

The same redact-identity scanner runs before any sync push or team share.

→

The rule doesn't relax as the architecture grows up. The RLM/MARL dispatch layer (v2, not yet built) runs against a local model or the user's own key, same as extraction does today — orchestration doesn't open a new door for code to leave the machine.

PROVENANCE · FULL BRAIN ANATOMY, MAPPED

Every structure has an address.

The design traces back to Frank Rosenblatt's 1961 Principles of Neurodynamics — the original perceptron paper — read as three unit types: sensory, association, response. Mapped structure by structure, the metaphor stops being decorative and becomes a build spec — for one repo's memory today, and for a whole company's nervous system in the slides ahead.

Two established algorithm families ground the "next" column, not just the metaphor: the motor-program layer is a recursive language model (RLM) — a model that decomposes one task into sub-calls of itself, the same shape a coding agent's own multi-step tool-calling loop already takes. The basal-ganglia dispatcher is formally a multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) problem — many procedures, one shared action space, competing for which one fires. Full mapping + the recall-vs-dispatch design consequence: docs/cognitive-runtime.md in the Entergram repo.

PROVENANCE · WHAT IT ACTUALLY CARRIED

Two and a half months. Here's what it carried.

Across those same ten weeks, the identical cells → index → recall loop was carrying real engineering weight, not toy examples. A multi-cloud infrastructure gateway shipped a rewrite that passed 137/137 tests in one pass, then landed a twelve-unit release — event validation, idempotency, quota enforcement, streaming — in a single tranche. Its automated harness now stands at 104/104 tests passing, with infrastructure-as-code coverage at 100% on one cloud, ~85% on a second, ~75% on a third.

A regulated workflow engine running underneath it went from a blended readiness score of 72.5 to 89.1 after a single contract rewrite, and now runs a live pool of 9 state machines, 24 execution units, and 5 data-sync rules across 18 live schemas.

Not every cell is a win, and that's the point. One of the oldest entries in the store is an honest negative: a health-signal mobile app's camera-based heart-rate model, validated against real people instead of a synthetic benchmark, came back ~33 BPM off a <5 BPM target. That uncomfortable number got written down, not buried — and it reshaped the entire product's direction. A live-streaming platform redeployed clean end-to-end under the same discipline.

A memory that only keeps the wins isn't a memory. It's a highlight reel.

THE NERVOUS SYSTEM, ORG-WIDE · THE LEADERSHIP LADDER AND ITS CADENCE

Individual to enterprise isn't one hop. Each rung has its own rhythm.

"Engineering leadership" on the last slide was one box standing in for a real ladder: a contributor reports to a lead, a lead to a manager, a manager to a VP or CDO, a VP to the COO, the COO to the CEO. Each rung meets at a different cadence — but every one of them is reading the same activity ledger, not preparing a separate status report for the level above.
